\section{Aim, research questions and hypotheses}\label{sec:aims}

\subsection{Aim}
The aim of the project is to \textbf{determine whether, and under which atmospheric conditions, the
UV-induced degradation of single-mode fibre end faces carrying a few milliwatts of \SI{369.5}{nm}
light can be stopped, and to demonstrate a low-cost, 3D-printed end-face enclosure that achieves this
in routine use}. The working hypothesis of the group is that removing oxygen from the end faces halts
the degradation. The project is designed so that it gives a clear answer whether this
hypothesis is right or wrong. If oxygen turns out not to be the culprit, the same
measurements identify what is (\cref{tab:predictions}).

\subsection{Research questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*},leftmargin=3.2em]
  \item \textbf{Where and what?} Is the throughput loss located at the input face, the output face or in
        the fibre, and what is its physical nature: a deposit, a restructured silica surface, colour
        centres, or misalignment?
  \item \textbf{Is oxygen the cause?} At fixed (low) humidity and contaminant levels, does removing
        \Oxy{} change the degradation rate, by how much and in which direction, and is there a threshold
        \Oxy{} concentration?
  \item \textbf{What else matters?} What are the separate roles of water vapour, organic and siloxane
        vapours (including those emitted by 3D-printed parts, adhesives and cable jackets), airborne
        particles and, optionally, hydrogen?
  \item \textbf{Does it work in practice?} Can a passive, sealed 3D-printed cell hold the required
        atmosphere for months, and does it stop the degradation of a working \SI{369.5}{nm} fibre link in
        one of the group's experiments?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Competing hypotheses}
The observations available so far constrain the mechanism considerably: high throughput after
re-polishing, a decline whose rate increases with power, and a decline at \emph{both} ends. Re-polishing
removes only micrometres to tens of micrometres of glass but fully restores the throughput, so the
damage sits at, or within micrometres of, the end faces. Bulk colour-centre formation along the fibre
would not be undone by polishing. The guided intensity is nearly the same at both faces, so a process
driven by the local optical intensity at a glass surface explains why the output face degrades too.
Mechanical misalignment of the input coupling does not. The literature reviewed in \cref{sec:sota} adds
a candidate the group may not have considered: the silica surface itself can restructure under CW blue
light~\cite{Gonschior2013JNCS}. Six hypotheses are retained (\cref{fig:mechanisms}):

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[H1 -- oxygen-assisted deposition or surface chemistry] Oxygen takes part in the damaging
        reaction. The best-documented route is the photo-oxidation of siloxanes, released by silicone
        greases, rubbers and adhesives, into non-volatile \ce{SiO_x} at the high-intensity spot. In a
        fibre-coupled \SI{410}{nm} module this was \emph{accelerated} above \SI{100}{ppm}
        \Oxy~\cite{Fujifilmpatent2006}. A second, more speculative route is the reaction of \Oxy{} with
        photo-activated sites on the polished silica. \emph{Prediction:} removing \Oxy{} slows or
        stops the degradation, and the rate rises with the \Oxy{} fraction. Any deposit is rich in Si and
        O and resists solvents, and a silicone source in the cell accelerates degradation far more in
        clean air than in nitrogen.
  \item[H2 -- carbonaceous laser-induced contamination] Hydrocarbons and plasticisers adsorbed from the
        gas phase are photo-fixed into an absorbing, carbon-rich film. In every community that has
        studied this, oxygen, and often water vapour, suppresses or removes such
        deposits~\cite{Hippler2016,Mondin2017,Weiller2010}. Growth can be fastest at \emph{trace} oxygen
        levels~\cite{Parthasarathy2024}. \emph{Prediction:} clean gas slows the degradation, but removing
        \Oxy{} while organics are present (e.g.\ outgassing from printed parts) makes it \emph{worse},
        especially at \SIrange{1}{1000}{ppm} residual \Oxy. Humid gas may help.
  \item[H3 -- restructuring of the silica surface] The glass surface itself develops nanostructures,
        for example laser-induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS) and a porous, lower-density silica
        layer. These have been found on both faces of single-mode fibres carrying CW \SI{405}{nm} light,
        with no foreign elements present~\cite{Gonschior2013JNCS,Gonschior2013PhD}. Adsorbed water or
        surface charging may assist the process~\cite{Gonschior2013PhD}. \emph{Prediction:} weak
        atmosphere dependence (perhaps slower in dry gas), a pronounced intensity threshold, and ripples
        aligned with the polarisation visible in SEM or AFM.
  \item[H4 -- particle accumulation] Airborne particles and oil droplets are pulled into the
        high-intensity spot by optical gradient forces and then burned into the surface~\cite{Marciniak2017}.
        \emph{Prediction:} any filtered gas helps, and discrete particles are visible on the end face.
  \item[H5 -- colour centres in the glass] Point defects form in the core within the polished-away
        depth, for example in a defect-rich layer left by polishing. \emph{Prediction:} no atmosphere
        effect, except a possible reduction in hydrogen-containing gas~\cite{Colombe2014}.
  \item[H6 -- mechanical or thermal artefact] Drift of the coupling optics, epoxy creep or ferrule movement.
        \emph{Prediction:} no atmosphere effect, and throughput is recovered by re-peaking the alignment without
        touching the fibre.
\end{description}
Several mechanisms may act together. H1 and H2 can even coexist, with oxygen helping against one
contaminant and harming with another. H2 and H4 are hard to separate in lab air, and any absorbing film
also heats the end face (\cref{app:estimates}), which can turn a slow process into a runaway one. The
experiment is therefore designed to measure the effect of each atmospheric factor separately, not just
to compare ``with'' and ``without'' oxygen.

\begin{keybox}[Why oxygen can only act at the surface]
At room temperature molecular oxygen is practically immobile in silica glass. With literature diffusion
data it penetrates no more than nanometres even in years (\cref{app:estimates}), so the external
atmosphere cannot change bulk solarisation of the core. Hydrogen is different: it diffuses through a
\SI{125}{\micro m} fibre within days~\cite{Lemaire1991}. Any oxygen effect must therefore act at the end
faces. The observed behaviour of the group's fibres (restored by re-polishing, both ends affected) points
to exactly such surface processes, which makes the project well motivated.
\end{keybox}
